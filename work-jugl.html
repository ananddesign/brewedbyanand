Case Study — JUGL

Your business, beautifully tracked.

A lightweight business OS for small sellers and pop-up businesses. JUGL is built around the loudest, hardest moment of a small business—the pop-up itself—where the owner is also the salesperson, cashier, inventory manager and accountant, all at once.

02 — It started with a pop-up

The selling was easy. Everything around it was broken.

My wife runs a small jewellery business and, like many small sellers, a lot of it happens at pop-ups. Watching her work, I noticed something that felt surprisingly broken: the actual selling was the easy part. The hard part was everything happening around it.

Stock had to be tracked ↓ Sales had to be recorded ↓ Cash and UPI had to be reconciled ↓ Customers and expenses had to be remembered ↓ Afterwards, someone had to figure out what happened

Most tools I found were built around “running a business” as an office activity. But small sellers don’t experience their business that way—they are the owner, salesperson, cashier, inventory manager and accountant, often at the same time.

That became the problem I wanted to solve.

03 — The opportunity

They don’t need an ERP. They need speed.

Thinking beyond my wife’s business, there are thousands of small sellers running jewellery, fashion, food and craft businesses through pop-ups, markets and small stores. They don’t need another complicated ERP. They need something that works at the speed of their business.

“A simple operating system for small businesses—especially when they’re out there selling.”

04 — Designing around the moment of sale

The most important screen wasn’t the dashboard.

The biggest insight was that the pivotal moment wasn’t the dashboard—it was the pop-up itself. When a customer is standing in front of you, there isn’t time to navigate a complicated system. So I structured JUGL around the complete business loop.

Prepare → Sell → Track → Understand

Before the pop-up

Add inventory, create the event, and know exactly what you’re taking with you.

During the pop-up

Log sales quickly, identify products, record quantity and payment method, keep a running total.

After the pop-up

Understand revenue, expenses, customers and what happened to your inventory.

The product therefore isn’t just an inventory manager. It’s an event-to-business system.

05 — One product, multiple jobs

Bringing the operational pieces together.

A small seller wears every hat, so JUGL had to cover every job—without ever feeling like six apps stitched together.

Inventory

Products, pricing, cost, margins, stock levels and SKUs.

Pop-ups

Create events, track stall costs, and manage upcoming, live and completed events.

Live selling

Scan or log a product manually, record quantity and payment method, keep a live sales log.

Expenses

Capture business expenses and import existing data through CSV.

Customers

Capture customer information at the point of sale, including through QR.

Checkout

A customer-facing QR checkout experience supporting UPI and cash.

06 — Making selling faster

Logging a sale shouldn’t mean scrolling a list.

One place I wanted to remove friction was logging a sale. Instead of forcing a seller to search through a long inventory list mid-conversation, JUGL introduces Scan Item.

Photograph the product → AI identifies the item → Seller verifies → Sale recorded

Because AI isn’t always right, low-confidence results are surfaced for manual verification rather than being silently trusted. That small interaction reflects a larger principle:

“Automate the tedious part. Keep the human in control.”

07 — The system behind the simplicity

A sale isn’t just a sale.

One sale touches many parts of the business. JUGL connects these pieces so sellers don’t have to maintain separate spreadsheets or remember what changed where.

Sale → Revenue → Payment method → Inventory → Customer → Event performance

The goal wasn’t to create more dashboards. It was to make the underlying business feel more connected.

09 — What I learned

The product had to almost disappear.

Building JUGL changed how I think about small-business products. The biggest opportunity isn’t always adding more functionality—it’s understanding when the product is being used, who is using it, and what else that person is doing at that exact moment.

For a small seller at a busy pop-up, the product has to almost disappear. It needs to help them sell—not make them manage software. And that started with something very personal:

I wasn’t researching a hypothetical user. I was watching someone close to me try to run a real business.

That made the problem impossible to ignore.
