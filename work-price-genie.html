Case Study — PhonePe Pincode

Price Genie: Shopping That Starts With a List, Not a Store

Traditional commerce asks users to pick a store before they know what it sells. Price Genie inverted that—users build a shopping list first, and the system finds the stores that can fulfill it.

01 — Context

Commerce built on open rails

Pincode is PhonePe’s hyperlocal shopping app, built on ONDC—India’s Open Network for Digital Commerce. Unlike a closed marketplace, ONDC connects thousands of independent local merchants through a shared protocol. Any store can list. Any buyer app can sell.

That openness is the network’s biggest strength and its biggest design problem. Inventory quality varies by merchant. Prices for the same item differ across stores. Availability changes hour to hour. And the interface most apps put on top of this chaos is the same one ecommerce has used for twenty years:

Pick a Store → Browse Products → Add to Cart → Checkout

Store-first discovery works when there are five stores. It breaks when there are five hundred. The user is forced to make their least-informed decision—which store?—before they have any information to make it with.

Image — Pincode app context: hyperlocal storefront grid showing dozens of nearby stores competing for the same first tap

The store-first entry point: hundreds of merchants, zero signal about which one has what you need.

02 — The Problem

Nobody wakes up wanting to shop from Store X

People wake up needing milk, bread, fruits, vegetables. The need is a list. The interface is a directory.

Watch someone do their weekly grocery run on a store-first app and the friction is obvious. They open a store, search for six items, find four. Now what? Abandon the two missing items, or open a second store and rebuild part of the cart from memory? Most people do neither—they settle, or they leave.

Users don’t think in stores. They think in products. We were asking them to translate.

Every store switch resets context: a new catalog, new prices, new delivery promises, a new cart. The cognitive work of comparison—the thing software is good at—was being pushed entirely onto the user.

Illustration — A user's mental model (a scribbled shopping list) vs. the app's model (a grid of store logos)

03 — The Challenge, From Four Sides

One broken flow, four unhappy parties

This wasn’t just a usability gap. Store-first discovery was quietly capping the marketplace itself.

Users

High effort to complete a full basket. No way to compare stores on the things they actually care about: availability, delivery time, savings, and whether the whole list gets fulfilled—not just price.

Business

Wanted higher discovery, higher cart conversion, and larger order values. Store-first flows produced small, single-store baskets and early drop-offs.

Merchants

Smaller stores with strong inventory in a few categories were invisible. Discovery rewarded brand recall, not fulfillment capability. Good merchants got no exposure.

Engineering

Matching a list against live inventory from hundreds of merchants—with changing prices, stock, and delivery promises—pushed against real API and offer-engine limitations.

Any solution had to work for all four at once. A flow users loved but merchants couldn’t serve, or a matching model the backend couldn’t compute in real time, would fail in production no matter how good it looked in Figma.

04 — My Role

Design as product strategy

I led end-to-end UX for the initiative—from the discovery research that shaped the product thesis, through information architecture and flows, to final UI and design QA at launch.

The parts that mattered most weren’t screens. They were decisions made in rooms with backend engineers about what could be computed, with the business team about what should be ranked, and with merchandising about how catalog quality would shape what users saw. On a project like this, interaction design and system feasibility are the same conversation.

05 — Research

What we learned before drawing anything

We combined user interviews, shopping-behavior observation, market analysis, and merchant-side data. Four insights ended up carrying the whole product.

01

Users shop items, not stores.

Shopping intent formed as a list of needs, almost never as a store preference. Store choice was a means, not a goal—and users resented having to make it first.

02

Store-first discovery creates unnecessary cognitive load.

Choosing a store before seeing inventory is a blind decision. Users compensated by defaulting to familiar names, which suppressed discovery for the entire long tail of merchants.

03

Cross-store comparison is where users give up.

Finding products across multiple stores meant rebuilding carts from memory. The moment a basket needed a second store, completion rates collapsed. This was the highest-friction point in the entire journey.

04

Price is not the decision. Fulfillment is.

Users said they wanted the cheapest option. They chose the store that had everything, delivered soon, and saved them a second order. Availability, delivery time, and total savings beat unit price—consistently. This insight named the product and shaped its ranking logic.

The pairing matters: cart conversion without fulfillment rate rewards overpromising. Order value without cancellation rate rewards matching users to stores that can’t deliver. Every metric had a counterweight.

07 — Framing

How might we let people shop the way they already think—starting from what they need, not where to get it?

08 — Exploration

Four models for discovery

Before committing, we prototyped four discovery models and tested them against the research insights, the metrics scorecard, and—critically—backend feasibility.

Option A — Store-first, improved

Rejected

Keep the existing model but add better store filters: ratings, delivery time, category depth. Lowest engineering cost, fastest to ship.

Rejected because it optimizes the wrong decision. Better filters make a blind choice slightly less blind—they don’t remove the translation work between “what I need” and “where to go.” The core insight said the sequence itself was broken.

Option B — Search-first

Rejected

A universal search bar across all merchant inventory. Type “milk,” see every store that sells milk.

Great for one item, wrong for a basket. Search resolves needs one at a time, so a six-item list means six searches and six store-comparison moments. It also depended on clean, consistent catalog data across merchants—which ONDC’s variable data quality couldn’t guarantee. Search stayed in the product, but as a utility, not the spine.

Option C — Category-first

Partially adopted

Users browse normalized categories—dairy, produce, staples—instead of stores, and the system aggregates items across merchants within each category.

This matched how users scan a mental shopping list, and normalized categories absorbed messy merchant data better than raw search. But on its own it still ended in a single-store cart. We kept category browsing as the entry point and pushed further.

Option D — Item-first (list-led)

Selected

Users build a shopping list first—via categories or search—without committing to any store. The system then matches the complete list against live merchant inventory and returns ranked stores by fulfillment, savings, and delivery.

This was the only model that moved comparison work from the user to the system, served whole baskets instead of single items, and gave long-tail merchants exposure based on what they could actually fulfill. It was also the hardest to build—which is exactly why the next sections are mostly about constraints.

09 — Product Architecture

Inverting the funnel

The architecture is a straight inversion of ecommerce’s standard sequence. Store selection moves from the first step—where the user has no information—to the fifth, where the system has all of it.

Browse Categories ↓ Add Items to List ↓ Review List ↓ Find Best Stores ↓ Compare Stores ↓ Create Cart ↓ Checkout

Two structural ideas hold this together.

The list is the primary object, not the cart. A cart belongs to a store. A list belongs to the user. Keeping the list store-agnostic until matching is what lets the same six items be priced against every eligible merchant simultaneously.

Matching is a moment, not a background process. “Find Best Stores” is an explicit, user-triggered step. Users understood the system was working on their behalf, and engineering got a single defined point to run the expensive inventory-matching computation—instead of recomputing on every list edit.

Diagram — System architecture: user list → matching engine → merchant inventory, price, and delivery-promise inputs → ranked store results

10 — Design Decisions

Six decisions that defined the product

Each chapter below follows the same arc: the problem, the reasoning, the tradeoff we accepted, and what happened.

Chapter 01

Categories: designing for a messy catalog

Problem
Merchant catalog data was inconsistent—the same milk brand appeared under different names, categories, and pack sizes across stores. Raw catalogs would make list-building feel broken before matching even ran.
Reasoning
List-building had to feel effortless because everything downstream depended on list quality. We designed around a normalized category taxonomy with the most-purchased essentials surfaced first, so users add generic items (“milk, 1L”) rather than store-specific SKUs.
Tradeoff
Normalization hides brand-level choice at the list stage. Brand-sensitive users pick specifics later, at store comparison. We accepted slightly less precision up front for much faster list completion.
Outcome
Users built larger lists faster, and the normalized layer became the contract that made cross-store matching computable at all.
Screen — Category browsing and quick-add: essentials grid with one-tap add to list

Chapter 02

Review List: the commitment moment

Problem
In testing, users treated their list casually—then felt ambushed when store results didn’t match expectations they hadn’t realized they had (quantities, sizes, substitutions).
Reasoning
We inserted an explicit review step before matching. It sets quantities, flags ambiguous items, and frames what happens next. It also protects the backend: matching runs once, on a confirmed list, not continuously on a changing one.
Tradeoff
One extra step in the flow—normally something you fight to remove. We kept it because it converted downstream confusion into upstream clarity, and cut abandoned matching results.
Outcome
Fewer surprises at comparison, fewer re-runs of matching, and a natural home for list editing on repeat visits.
Screen — Review List: editable quantities, ambiguity flags, and the “Find Best Stores” action

Chapter 03

Store Matching: ranking on fulfillment, not price

Problem
How should matched stores be ordered? The obvious answer—cheapest first—contradicted our own research: users chose completeness and delivery over unit price.
Reasoning
We ranked primarily on fulfillment (how much of the list a store covers), with savings and delivery time as secondary signals. A store with 9/10 items at slightly higher prices beats a store with 6/10 items at the lowest prices, because the real cost of a missing item is a second order.
Tradeoff
A cheapest-first sort would have demoed better. Fulfillment-first occasionally puts a pricier store on top and requires the UI to justify why—which is exactly what the fulfillment snapshot (next chapter) exists to do.
Outcome
Lower cancellation and second-order rates in pilot, and merchant exposure shifted toward stores that could actually serve the basket—including small merchants with deep inventory in narrow categories.
Screen — Store matching results: ranked stores with fulfillment count, total price, savings, and delivery promise

Chapter 04

Fulfillment Snapshot: honest comparison at a glance

Problem
Comparing stores across four dimensions—coverage, price, savings, delivery—risked becoming a spreadsheet. Dense comparison tables tested poorly; users wanted a verdict, then evidence.
Reasoning
Each store got a “fulfillment snapshot”: items available out of items requested, stated plainly (“9 of 10 items”), with missing items named—not hidden. Being explicit about gaps was a deliberate trust decision. A store that hides what it lacks earns a cancellation later.
Tradeoff
Showing missing items makes some stores look worse and can cost a conversion in the moment. We traded short-term conversion for fulfillment integrity, because cancellations were the more expensive failure.
Outcome
The snapshot became the most-referenced element in usability sessions—users made confident store choices in seconds and could articulate why.
Screen — Fulfillment snapshot card, expanded state with missing items listed
Wireframe — Rejected comparison-table exploration, kept for contrast

Chapter 05

Checkout: from list to cart without loss

Problem
Once a user picks a store, their generic list must convert into that store’s actual SKUs, prices, and stock—which may have changed since matching ran. Silent substitutions here would destroy the trust the snapshot built.
Reasoning
Cart creation shows a reconciliation view: what mapped cleanly, what changed, what needs a decision. The user confirms deltas, not the whole cart. Anything the store can’t serve returns to the list, ready for a follow-up match instead of disappearing.
Tradeoff
A reconciliation step is slower than one-tap cart creation. Given dynamic inventory on ONDC, we chose accuracy at the moment of commitment over speed—this is where money changes hands.
Outcome
Faster overall completion despite the extra confirmation, because users stopped re-checking carts item by item. Time to checkout dropped measurably in pilot.
Screen — Cart creation: reconciliation view showing matched items, price deltas, and unserved items returning to the list

Chapter 06

Offer Engine: savings users can believe

Problem
The offer engine computed discounts per store, per item, with restrictions on stacking and timing—and couldn’t always guarantee an offer at matching time would hold at checkout.
Reasoning
We surfaced savings at the basket level (“you save ₹X on this list here”) rather than itemized coupon mechanics, and only displayed offers the engine could commit to. A smaller number that holds beats a bigger number that evaporates at payment.
Tradeoff
Understating potential savings made some stores look less competitive than they might be. We accepted a conservative number in exchange for zero checkout-time surprises.
Outcome
Savings became a trusted comparison signal instead of a fine-print gamble, reinforcing the fulfillment-first ranking story.

11 — Engineering Collaboration

Designing inside real constraints

Every major interaction in Price Genie was shaped by a backend conversation. The constraints weren’t obstacles to the design—they were inputs to it.

Matching was expensive, so matching became a moment. Live inventory lookups across many merchants couldn’t run on every list edit. That constraint produced the explicit “Find Best Stores” step—which then tested better than continuous background matching, because users could see the system working for them.

Data quality varied, so the taxonomy became the contract. The normalized category layer wasn’t just a browsing convenience. It was the agreement between design and backend about what a “match” even means when two merchants describe the same product differently.

Promises were dynamic, so the UI stopped pretending otherwise. Delivery estimates and stock could shift between matching and checkout. Instead of designing around a fiction of static data, the reconciliation step made change a first-class state with a clear recovery path.

The interaction model is the API constraint, made humane. That’s why product design needed a seat in backend discussions—not a handoff at the end of them.

12 — Pilot

A closed rollout, on purpose

Price Genie launched as a closed pilot in select serviceable areas with a curated merchant set. The bar for expansion was the scorecard: fulfillment rate and cancellation rate had to hold before we widened the funnel. A flow that converts but doesn’t deliver is just a faster way to disappoint people.

What we observed

Users built noticeably larger lists than the carts they built in store-first flows—when adding an item doesn’t commit you to a store, adding is cheap. The fulfillment snapshot did the persuasion work we’d hoped: store choice was fast and rarely reversed. The weakest link was catalog normalization at the edges—ambiguous items (“paneer” vs. a specific brand and weight) produced the most matching complaints.

What we iterated

We tightened ambiguity flags in Review List, tuned how aggressively matching relaxed pack-size constraints, and simplified the comparison card after watching users ignore its second row of metadata. The roadmap beyond pilot: reorder from a saved list, multi-store split baskets for lists no single merchant could serve, and personalization of the category grid.

13 — Outcomes

What changed

Pilot numbers are directional—a closed rollout with a curated merchant set isn’t a clean A/B test—but the direction was consistent across cohorts.

+15%
Average items added

Decoupling the list from the store made adding items cheap. Bigger lists are the input to every downstream business metric.

+20%
Product discovery

Category-led browsing exposed users to items and merchants they never encountered in store-first sessions.

+2%
Cart conversion

Modest by design—fulfillment-first ranking deliberately sacrifices some conversion for orders that actually complete.

+18%
Faster shopping completion

One matching moment replaced serial store-hopping. Users spent their time choosing, not searching.

For users, the comparison work moved into the system: higher fulfillment, fewer second orders, less cognitive load.

For merchants, exposure started tracking inventory capability instead of brand recall—small stores with deep stock in narrow categories won baskets they’d never have been found for.

For engineering and the product org, the list-matching architecture and the normalized taxonomy outlived the experiment: both were reused by later storefront experiences on the platform. That’s the quiet outcome I’m proudest of—experiments that leave infrastructure behind pay for themselves even if the feature evolves.

14 — Reflection

What I’d do differently now

Start with the reconciliation problem, not the discovery problem. We designed forward from list-building and discovered late that cart reconciliation was where trust was won or lost. Today I’d prototype the hardest moment of truth first and design backward from it.

Treat catalog quality as a design surface earlier. We inherited normalization as a backend concern. It turned out to be the single biggest determinant of experience quality. I’d now put a designer in the taxonomy discussions from week one.

Where AI changes this product. The list is a natural language object—“stuff for Saturday breakfast for six” should become a structured, quantified list in one step. LLMs also solve the exact problem that hurt us most: reconciling messy merchant catalog entries against a normalized taxonomy is a language problem, and it’s now a largely solved one. Matching explanations (“this store ranks first because…”) could be generated per user, per basket.

How personalization would work. Grocery lists are heavily repeated. A remembered list that pre-builds itself, ranking that learns whether this user trades price for completeness, and a category grid ordered by household patterns—all of it sits naturally on the list-first architecture, because the list is a durable user-owned object in a way a store-bound cart never was.

The lesson that stayed with me: the biggest UX gains come from reordering decisions, not redesigning screens. We didn’t make store choice easier. We moved it to where it was easy.

Price Genie shaped how I approach every product since: find the decision the user is least equipped to make, and ask whether the system should be making it instead.
