Essay — Design Tools / Product Thinking

Building a Small Business App Changed How I Think About Design Tools

Working on a small business app forces clarity. No luxury of overthinking flows. You’re solving real operational problems. That’s what pushed me deeper into code-first design experimentation.

2026

Working on a small business app forces clarity.

No luxury of endless design reviews. No beautifully staged handoff rituals. No time to admire wireframes.

You’re solving operational problems—orders, workflows, tracking, merchant actions. The product doesn’t need theatre. It needs to work.

That reality pushed me into experimenting with code-first design and AI-native workflows while building this product. And it changed how I think about design tooling.

1. For Small Business Apps, Code-First Feels Incredibly Logical

In a small business app, utility beats polish in the early stages. You care less about pixel-perfect emotional storytelling and more about whether a merchant can finish a task without friction.

That makes designing in code surprisingly attractive.

Instead of: Idea → Figma → Prototype → Handoff → Build

You get: Idea → Build → Interact immediately

That compression changes everything. You’re not imagining the flow. You’re feeling it.

You can test actual interactions, real constraints, real responsiveness—much earlier. For this category, code-first doesn’t feel experimental. It feels practical.

2. Canvas Is Still the Fastest Way to Explore

But designing isn’t just building. It’s exploring. And exploration is chaotic.

Move the CTA here. Try filters on top. Shrink this section. Kill this card. Actually bring it back.

This kind of iteration is instinctive. Visual. Fast. Canvas still dominates this phase.

Because the moment every micro decision has to be translated into language or implementation, speed collapses. Dragging a block takes seconds. Describing that same intent takes thought, then interpretation, then retries.

AI speeds up execution. Canvas still speeds up thinking. Those are not the same thing.

3. Designing in Code Creates Hidden Double Work

The strongest advantage of designing in code is realism. You see the actual product immediately. Not simulated interactions. Not designer approximations. The real thing.

But realism has a hidden tax. Design refinement gets entangled with engineering friction. Spacing tweaks become implementation work. Layout experiments break responsiveness. Simple visual improvements turn into debugging sessions.

Good product design lives in subtle details: hierarchy, spacing rhythm, visual clarity, micro interactions, whitespace balance. Those decisions are lightweight in design tools. Heavier in code.

You move faster toward something functional. Sometimes slower toward something refined.

4. Claude Design Is Impressive, But Still Feels Naive About Design Work

I spent meaningful time experimenting with Claude Design while building this app. And the first impression is genuinely impressive. You can describe a screen, edit through conversation, refine components through prompts. That feels magical.

At one point, I gave it a Pinterest UI reference—and it recreated the direction surprisingly well. Messy, yes. But useful. Especially when you need fast inspiration or rough scaffolding.

But the cracks show quickly.

For example, I nearly spent 20 minutes fixing padding between two CTA buttons. Not because the problem was hard. Because communicating precision through prompts becomes absurdly inefficient.

A designer would fix that in seconds on a canvas. Drag. Adjust. Done. Instead it became: describe spacing, wait, review, prompt again, fix side effects, repeat.

Not every visual decision should become a conversation. Sometimes language is the slowest possible interface.

Claude understands structure better than nuance. It can imitate references, but originality is weak. It can generate interfaces, but design judgment is still shallow.

5. The Real Future Is Smart Canvas

This whole experiment made one thing obvious: the future isn’t no-canvas design. That framing is wrong.

The real opportunity is smart canvas.

A workspace where visual edits happen instantly, AI helps with generation and repetitive work, code previews stay live, references can become starting points, and layout changes don’t require linguistic negotiation.

A hybrid creation environment. Because designers don’t think in one mode—sometimes visually, sometimes structurally, sometimes conversationally. Great tools should adapt to that. Not force one interface for every problem.

Building this small business app taught me something simple:

The future of design tools isn’t AI replacing canvas.

It’s AI making canvas intelligent.

Always brewing something new
