02 Context

Okay, so what’s ONDC and Pincode?

Launched by the Government of India, ONDC enables different e-commerce platforms to communicate with each other and creates an open, interoperable marketplace.

It also standardises operations like cataloguing, inventory management and order fulfilment, making e-commerce simpler and more accessible. Pincode is the 0→1 app built on it.

06 Findings

What we found was…

  • Anxiety of delivery charges being offered in next steps
  • Users frequently requested refunds or cancellations due to errors in their orders
  • Customers kept raising tickets of “where is my order?”
  • Users contacted customer service more frequently
  • Users were unable to relate offers to the cart page
  • Users were thinking Pincode was delivering the order
  • The time shown to deliver was the bigger surprise
  • Users struggled to understand vague order statuses like “Processing” or “Pending”
  • Users were anxious about the order status
  • Due to confusion, users left the cart midway
  • Users double-checked payment confirmations
  • ETA was set the same as quick commerce, so the experience dropped suddenly

10 Decisions

Key decisions & trade-offs

The checkout experience needed to reduce uncertainty without losing the context users needed to make informed decisions. These were the key design decisions that shaped the experience.

01

Keep the originating store visible

The challenge
Users could be unclear about which store was fulfilling their order and who was responsible for it. Pincode operated as a marketplace rather than owning the storefront or inventory.
Options considered
  • Keep Pincode as the dominant identity throughout the journey.
  • Emphasise the delivery partner as the primary point of reference.
  • Make the originating store more visible across the order journey.
Decision
Reinforce the store identity so users could better understand where their order originated and who was fulfilling it.
Trade-off
This meant giving more prominence to the store within a Pincode experience. The aim was to make fulfilment ownership clearer without obscuring Pincode’s role as the marketplace.
02

Communicate order progress within integration constraints

The challenge
Third-party integration limitations meant that delivery-partner details, such as the rider’s name and photo, were not always available.
Options considered
  • Continue relying on generic status labels.
  • Wait until richer delivery-partner information could be made available.
  • Improve the progress experience using the order information already available.
Decision
Make order progress and expectations clearer through more meaningful status communication and accessible support actions.
Trade-off
The experience could not provide complete delivery-person transparency in every case. Instead, it focused on reducing uncertainty with the information and controls available.
03

Make delivery information easier to understand

The challenge
Delivery charges, timelines and options could create uncertainty during checkout.
Options considered
  • Present all delivery information together upfront.
  • Defer some details to a later step.
  • Improve the hierarchy so the most important delivery information was easier to scan, with supporting details available when needed.
Decision
Refine the presentation and hierarchy of delivery information to help users understand the implications of their choices before placing an order.
Trade-off
The design needed to balance a simpler checkout with enough information for users to make an informed decision.

11 Iterations

Iterations: from friction to clarity

These iterations show how the experience evolved in response to the issues identified.

01

Rebalancing the cart hierarchy

What wasn’t working
The store information was taking up significant visual attention, reducing the prominence of the items users had added to their cart.
What changed
The cart hierarchy was refined to bring the added items back into focus while retaining the store context users needed.
Why
The cart’s primary job is to help users review what they intend to purchase. Store context is useful, but it should not compete with that task.
What we learned
Supporting information needs to be visible without overpowering the user’s primary task.
02

Clarifying delivery options and information

What wasn’t working
Users were dropping off while viewing delivery options, suggesting that the information or choice presentation was creating friction.
What changed
The delivery information and options were reorganised to make the important details easier to understand and compare.
Why
Users need to understand delivery implications, such as timing and charges, before they commit to an order.
What we learned
Reducing friction is not simply about removing information. It is about presenting the right information at the point where it helps users make a decision.
03

Making order tracking more reassuring

What wasn’t working
Generic order statuses did not always explain what was happening or what users should expect next. Third-party integration constraints also limited the delivery-partner information available.
What changed
The tracking experience was refined to communicate order progress more clearly and make relevant support actions easier to access.
Why
When fulfilment is distributed across stores and delivery partners, users need clarity about progress and where to seek help.
What we learned
A useful tracking experience is not only about showing a status. It should set expectations and help users understand what they can do next.

Iteration takeaway

Across the cart, delivery and tracking experiences, the direction was consistent: preserve the information users need, reduce competing visual noise, and make the next step easier to understand.
